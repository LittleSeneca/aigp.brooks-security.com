# Verified case library: Domain II.C and II.D

**Purpose.** Worked, citable cases for the non-narrative book, aligned to BOK v2.1 competencies II.C (main elements of AI-specific laws) and II.D (standards and frameworks). Every row was checked against the source in the final column during preparation. Nothing here relies on memory alone.

**Status of the law.** Verified as of 27 September 2026.

**How to read the table.** One row per instrument or case. "BOK competency" gives the primary mapping, and several rows also serve the other competency (for example, the EU harmonised standards row is a II.D case that sits inside a II.C conformity-assessment discussion, and the AI Act risk tiering row is a II.C case that depends on the II.D definition of an AI system).

| EU Artificial Intelligence Act | Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 of the European Parliament and of the Council | Adopted 13 June 2024; published in the Official Journal 12 July 2024; in force 1 August 2024 | https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng | II.C: AI-specific law, risk classification | The Act sorts AI systems into prohibited, high-risk, limited-risk and minimal-risk tiers and attaches duties to the use rather than the technology. High-risk status is reached by two routes, Annex I (a safety component of a product under EU harmonisation law) and Annex III (a closed list of uses that includes biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services including credit scoring, law enforcement, migration and justice). Article 6(3) lets a listed system escape the tier where it poses no significant risk, but that exit is never available to a system that profiles natural persons. Article 27 adds a fundamental rights impact assessment for certain deployers. The official consolidated text is the authority; the current consolidation is dated 27 July 2026. |
| Digital Omnibus on AI | Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 8 July 2026 amending Regulations (EU) 2024/1689, (EU) 2018/1139 and (EU) 2023/1230 as regards the simplification of the implementation of harmonised rules on artificial intelligence | Adopted 8 July 2026; published 24 July 2026; in force 27 July 2026 | https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng | II.C: AI-specific law, amendment and transitional dates | The first amendment to the AI Act. It defers the standalone Annex III high-risk rules from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 and the Annex I embedded high-risk rules to 2 August 2028, and it sets 2 December 2026 as the Article 50(2) marking deadline for generative systems already on the market before 2 August 2026. It also adds a legal basis (Article 4a) for processing special-category personal data for bias work, softens the Article 4 literacy duty to a duty to take supportive measures, extends simplified treatment to small mid-caps, and gives the AI Office market-surveillance and enforcement powers over certain systems. Verified from the Official Journal text and from the Commission's official AI Act timeline at https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/timeline/timeline-implementation-eu-ai-act |
| AI Act prohibited practices | Article 5, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 | In force since 2 February 2025 | https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-5 | II.C: prohibited practices and enforcement | Article 5 bans a closed list of practices outright: manipulative or subliminal techniques that distort behaviour, exploitation of vulnerabilities, social scoring, individual criminal-offence prediction based solely on profiling, untargeted scraping of facial images, emotion recognition in workplaces and educational institutions, biometric categorisation by sensitive traits, and real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces subject to narrow law-enforcement exceptions. Emotion recognition outside those settings is not banned and instead carries a deployer disclosure duty under Article 50. The Digital Omnibus added two further prohibitions from 2 December 2026, one on intimate imagery generated without consent and one on child sexual abuse material. |
| AI Act conformity assessment and certification in practice | Article 43, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689; ISO/IEC 42006:2025; EN 18286:2026 | Article 43 as enacted 2024; ISO/IEC 42006 published 2025; prEN 18286 approved October 2025 as the first harmonised AI standard, published as EN 18286:2026 | https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-43 | II.C: conformity assessment; II.D: certification and conformity assessment in practice | Conformity assessment for most Annex III systems runs by internal control without a notified body, while Annex I product-embedded systems and cases where harmonised standards are not applied bring a notified body in. The Digital Omnibus made a technical correction to Article 43(3) and clarified that embedding a high-risk AI system in a product does not by itself force third-party assessment. ISO/IEC 42006:2025 sets the additional requirements for the bodies that audit and certify AI management systems, and it builds on ISO/IEC 17021-1. EN 18286, a quality management system standard for AI Act regulatory purposes, is the first harmonised standard produced for the Act by CEN-CENELEC JTC 21. Presumption of conformity attaches only once a harmonised standard is cited in the Official Journal. Sources: ISO catalogue https://www.iso.org/standard/42006 and CEN-CENELEC https://www.cencenelec.eu/news-events/news/2026/en-in-the-spotlight/2026-07-30-ai-quality-management/ |
| AI Act transparency duties | Article 50, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, with the Commission's Article 50 guidelines and the Code of Practice on marking and labelling of AI-generated content | Guidelines published 20 July 2026; marking code published 10 June 2026; obligations apply from 2 August 2026 | https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-50 | II.C: transparency obligations | Article 50 splits the duties by role. Deployers must disclose deepfakes and their use of emotion recognition or biometric categorisation, and must flag AI-generated text published on matters of public interest unless it has been reviewed under editorial responsibility. The Commission published implementing guidance on 20 July 2026 and a voluntary marking and labelling code on 10 June 2026. Sources: https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/guidelines-transparency-obligations-providers-and-deployers-ai-systems and https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-publishes-code-practice-marking-and-labelling-ai-generated-content |
| AI Act general-purpose AI duties and the GPAI Code of Practice | Article 53 and Article 54, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689; General-Purpose AI Code of Practice | GPAI obligations apply from 2 August 2025; code published 10 July 2025 with adequacy confirmed 1 August 2025 | https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-53 | II.C: general-purpose AI duties | Every provider of a general-purpose AI model owes technical documentation, information that lets downstream providers understand the model's capabilities and limits, a copyright policy that honours text and data mining reservations, and a public summary of training content. Non-EU providers must appoint an authorised representative in the Union. The GPAI Code of Practice is a voluntary tool prepared by independent experts that gives signatories a practical route to demonstrate compliance with Articles 53 and 55; adherence is evidence of compliance, while a legal presumption of conformity is reserved for harmonised standards. The Commission maintains the signatory list. Source: https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/contents-code-gpai |
| AI Act penalties | Article 99 and Article 101, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 | Penalty regime applies from 2 August 2025, with the Commission's GPAI fining powers from 2 August 2026 | https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-99 | II.C: penalties and enforcement | Article 99 sets three ceilings, each the higher of a fixed sum or a percentage of total worldwide annual turnover: EUR 35 million or 7 percent for breaching the Article 5 prohibitions, EUR 15 million or 3 percent for most other obligations, and EUR 7.5 million or 1 percent for supplying incorrect, incomplete or misleading information to authorities. | South Korea AI Framework Act | Framework Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and the Creation of a Foundation for Trust, Act No. 20676 | Promulgated 21 January 2025; in force 22 January 2026 | https://elaw.klri.re.kr/eng_service/lawView.do?hseq=71019&lang=ENG | II.C: AI-specific law outside the EU | Korea's approach is a single umbrella statute that puts promotion first and regulates through overlapping categories instead of a prohibited and high-risk ladder. Unlike the EU Act it creates no prohibited practices, no CE marking, no notified-body conformity assessment and no registration database. The KLRI published English text is the government-funded authoritative translation, and the official title differs from the short form "AI Basic Act" used in commentary. |

The Federal Trade Commission enforces the platform duties. Source for the enforcement position: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-take-it-down-act |
| CFPB circulars on adverse action and algorithmic decisions, and their withdrawal | Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-03; Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2023-03; withdrawal published at 90 FR (12 May 2025) | Circulars issued June 2022 and May 2023; withdrawn 12 May 2025 | https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/05/12/2025-08286/interpretive-rules-policy-statements-and-advisory-opinions-withdrawal | II.C: US federal guidance and its volatility | The CFPB's circulars took the position that creditors using complex algorithms must still give specific and accurate adverse action reasons, and that a sample form must not be used where it obscures the real reason. In May 2025 the Bureau withdrew these and other interpretive rules and circulars, so the interpretive position no longer stands as Bureau guidance even though the underlying adverse action statute and Regulation B still apply. It is the clearest illustration that US AI governance at federal level is guidance rather than enacted AI-specific law, and that guidance is reversible without legislation. Confirmed list of withdrawals: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/guidance/withdrawn-guidance/ |
| Colorado automated decision-making technology law | Senate Bill 26-189, repealing and reenacting Senate Bill 24-205 | SB 26-189 signed 14 May 2026 and operative 1 January 2027; SB 24-205's operative date had been moved from 1 February 2026 to 30 June 2026 by SB 25B-004 | https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189 | II.C: US state AI law and the Colorado trap | Colorado was the first state to enact a broad AI discrimination law, and it is the best example of a law that was replaced before it ever operated. SB 24-205 imposed a developer duty of reasonable care and impact assessments; SB 25B-004 postponed its operative date; SB 26-189 then repealed and reenacted the regime as an automated decision-making technology law with technical documentation, pre-use notice, post-adverse-outcome disclosure, data correction and meaningful human review, enforced only by the attorney general under the consumer protection statute. | Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act | House Bill 149, Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act (TRAIGA) | Signed 22 June 2025; effective 1 January 2026 | https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/HB00149F.htm | II.C: US state AI law, intent versus effect | TRAIGA regulates conduct rather than uses, banning AI developed or deployed with intent to discriminate, incite harm or infringe rights, and it treats disparate impact alone as insufficient. Its government provisions add rules on social scoring and biometric identifiers, and it recognises substantial compliance with named frameworks as a defence, which is the clearest example of a voluntary standard being given legal weight. It also contains an explicit safe harbour for federally insured financial institutions complying with banking law. Enforced by the Texas attorney general. | New York City Local Law 144 | Local Law 144 of 2021, automated employment decision tools, enforced by the New York City Department of Consumer and Worker Protection | Enacted 2021; DCWP enforcement began 5 July 2023 | https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/about/automated-employment-decision-tools.page | II.C: US local AI law, audit-based regulation | Local Law 144 prohibits employers and employment agencies from using an automated employment decision tool unless it has been subject to a bias audit within one year of use, information about the audit is publicly available, and required notices have gone to candidates or employees. It is the earliest operational US AI audit requirement and the model that later state laws drew on. |

The older AI Video Interview Act regulates the other side of the same transactions, requiring employers who use AI to analyse applicant-submitted video to give advance notice, explain how the analysis works, obtain consent, and limit distribution and retention. | OECD AI Principles | Recommendation of the Council on Artificial Intelligence, OECD/LEGAL/0449 | Adopted 22 May 2019; AI system definition revised 8 November 2023; Recommendation revised 3 May 2024 | https://legalinstruments.oecd.org/en/instruments/OECD-LEGAL-0449 | II.D: standards and frameworks, values baseline | The first intergovernmental standard on AI and the source of the definition almost every later instrument borrows. It carries five values-based principles for AI actors and five recommendations to policy makers, and it is not binding on the adherents that commit to implement it politically. The 2024 revision added explicit language on information integrity, misuse and sustainability while keeping five principles. The 2023 definition, which turns on inference from input toward outputs that influence physical or virtual environments, is closely tracked by Article 3(1) of the EU AI Act, so a voluntary text now marks the boundary of a binding law. |
| NIST AI Risk Management Framework and its Playbook | Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0), NIST AI 100-1, and the companion AI RMF Playbook | AI RMF 1.0 published January 2023; Playbook maintained as a companion resource | https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf | II.D: standards and frameworks, risk process | The US government's voluntary, sector-agnostic risk framework, directed by Congress under the National AI Initiative Act. Its normative content sits in four functions, GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE, with GOVERN cross-cutting, and it names seven trustworthiness characteristics including validity and reliability, safety, security and resilience, accountability and transparency, explainability, privacy enhancement, and fairness with harmful bias managed. It is a framework rather than a standard and there is no certification against it, which is the distinction most often tested. The Playbook supplies suggested actions, documentation prompts and references for each subcategory and is a separate how-to layer, not a second framework. Source: https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/playbook/ |
| NIST Generative AI Profile | NIST AI 600-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile | Published 26 July 2024 | https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf | II.D: standards and frameworks, profiles | A cross-sectoral profile that implements the AI RMF for generative AI, adding suggested actions under the same functions and subcategories rather than replacing the framework. It names twelve risks that generative AI creates or worsens, including confabulation, harmful bias and homogenization, information integrity, data privacy, information security and value chain integration, and it frames four primary considerations covering governance, pre-deployment testing, content provenance and incident disclosure. It was produced pursuant to Executive Order 14110, which was later revoked, while the profile remains published. Texas's TRAIGA expressly recognises substantial compliance with it as a defence, which is the clearest example of a voluntary profile carrying legal weight. |
| AI vocabulary standard | ISO/IEC 22989:2022, Information technology, Artificial intelligence, Artificial intelligence concepts and terminology | Published 2022 by ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 | https://www.iso.org/standard/74296.html | II.D: standards and frameworks, terminology | The shared vocabulary that the other AI standards build on. It defines on the order of a hundred concepts, including the split between AI as a discipline and an AI system as an engineered artifact, and it supplies the role terms (AI provider, AI manufacturer, AI customer, AI partner, AI subject) that deliberately do not match the EU AI Act's provider, deployer, importer and distributor. Its trustworthiness vocabulary carries the distinctions that exam items turn on, such as robustness against resilience, reliability against accuracy, and explainability against transparency. It is the one core ISO AI standard published free of charge, and both 42001 and 23894 reference its life cycle. |
| AI management system standard | ISO/IEC 42001:2023, Information technology, Artificial intelligence, Management system | Published December 2023, first edition | https://www.iso.org/standard/42001 | II.D: standards and frameworks, certifiable management system | The first certifiable AI management system standard, which is what separates it from the guidance standards. It follows the Annex SL high-level structure common to ISO management systems and runs on a Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle, and it requires an AI system impact assessment process in clause 6.1.4 and its performance in clause 8.4, with the controls in Annex A objective A.5. Certification is a contract and market expectation rather than a legal requirement, and it gives no presumption of conformity with the EU AI Act and does not by itself satisfy the Article 17 quality management system duty. What is certified is the organization's management system, not a particular AI system. |
| AI system impact assessment guidance | ISO/IEC 42005:2025, Information technology, Artificial intelligence, AI system impact assessment | Published 28 May 2025, first edition | https://www.iso.org/standard/42005 | II.D: standards and frameworks, impact assessment | The companion guidance that fills the gap 42001 leaves: 42001 requires an impact assessment process, and 42005 explains how to design and run one. It covers the process, from scope, timing, responsibilities and impact scales through performance, analysis, approval and review, and the record, covering system description, data, models, deployment environment, interested parties, benefits and harms including foreseeable misuse, and measures. It is guidance only, nothing is certified against it, and because it is not a harmonised standard it gives no presumption of conformity under the AI Act. A 42005 assessment can inform a GDPR data protection impact assessment or an AI Act Article 27 fundamental rights impact assessment but replaces neither. |
| AI risk management guidance | ISO/IEC 23894:2023, Information technology, Artificial intelligence, Guidance on risk management | Published 2023, first edition | https://www.iso.org/standard/77304.html | II.D: standards and frameworks, organizational risk | Guidance on managing risk related to AI, built on ISO 31000 and its definition of risk as the effect of uncertainty on objectives. It keeps the generic process of establishing context, identifying, analysing, evaluating, treating, monitoring and reviewing, and adds the AI-specific risk sources, the mapping of the process onto the AI system life cycle, and the governance and accountability arrangements AI risk needs. It details the risk process that 42001 holds as one of its management system processes, and it asks about risk to the organization where 42005 asks about impact on people. It is guidance and not certifiable, and it is not one of the three core ISO standards named in BOK v2.1 II.D. |

## UNVERIFIED, excluded

Kept out of the main table because no authoritative source could be found, or because a primary source contradicts the claim. Each was investigated, not merely left unread.

1. **EU AI Act first fines.** A secondary aggregator reports first AI Act fines in March 2026 totalling about EUR 85 million. No official European Commission, AI Office or national authority source confirms any AI Act fine. Excluded. The manuscript's assertion that no AI Act fine had been publicly announced as of September 2026 was not contradicted by any official source, but it could not be positively confirmed either.
2. **South Korea amendment by Act No. 21311 of 20 January 2026.** The manuscript asserts this amending act. The official KLRI English text of Act No. 20676 shows only a commencement provision deferring the digital medical device element of the definition to 24 January 2026. No government source confirming Act No. 21311 was found in this pass. Excluded.
3. **South Korean thresholds and figures.** The KRW 30 million administrative fine cap, the KRW 1 trillion and KRW 10 billion representative thresholds, the one million daily user threshold, the 10^26 FLOP "high-performance" bar, and the MSIT one-year enforcement grace period could not be confirmed from the official Act or Enforcement Decree text in this pass. Excluded pending a read of the Enforcement Decree itself.
4. **GPAI Code of Practice counts and signatory composition.** The figure of 21 providers signed to the full Code, the claim that xAI signed only the Safety and Security chapter, the claim that Meta is absent, and the roughly 190 signatories to the marking and labelling code could not be confirmed against the Commission's live signatory list in this pass. The existence and status of both codes is verified; the counts are excluded.
5. **ISO/IEC 42006 date.** The lead given as ISO/IEC 42006:2024 is contradicted by the ISO catalogue, which lists ISO/IEC 42006:2025. The 2025 date is used.
6. **NIST AI RMF Playbook JSON endpoint.** The machine-readable JSON endpoint is asserted as public domain. The Playbook page is JavaScript-driven and the JSON could not be retrieved or inspected, so no claim about the JSON is made.
7. **NIST AI 100-2 (adversarial machine learning taxonomy).** The report exists in both an E2023 and an E2025 version, but no row was written because the edition and date intended by the brief is ambiguous and the taxonomy content was not read. Verified to exist at https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ai/100/2/e2025/final
8. **New York RAISE Act, Executive Order 14110.** Both were verified to exist (RAISE Act signed 19 December 2025 per the Governor's office; EO 14110 revoked 20 January 2025 and named in NIST AI 600-1 as its authority). Neither is in the main table because the coverage list did not require a second frontier-safety or federal-order row and a row was not fetched end to end.

## Notes for the manuscript

- **Digital Omnibus verification result: the claim holds.** Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 exists, is titled as the "Digital Omnibus on AI", amends Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and enters into force on the third day after publication, that is 27 July 2026. The Annex III high-risk application date moves from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 and the Annex I date to 2 August 2028, confirmed independently by the Official Journal text and the Commission's official AI Act Service Desk implementation timeline. The two new Article 5 prohibitions from 2 December 2026 and the 2 December 2026 deadline for Article 50(2) marking of legacy generative systems are also confirmed.
- **Colorado claim holds.** SB 24-205 never became operative, so the "never effective" framing is correct. The mechanism is worth stating: SB 25B-004 postponed the operative date from 1 February 2026 to 30 June 2026, and SB 26-189 repealed and reenacted the regime before that date arrived.
- **Title correction.** The Korean statute's official English title is the Framework Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and the Creation of a Foundation for Trust. "AI Basic Act" is a short form used in commentary, not the formal title.
- **Coverage gap.** The brief's target of 14 to 20 cases could not be met while covering every named instrument, because the standards block alone names nine items and the EU AI Act block seven. The table above carries 24 rows. The four ISO items in the plan are all present, with ISO/IEC 42006 and the EU harmonised standard EN 18286 folded into the conformity-assessment row rather than given rows of their own.
