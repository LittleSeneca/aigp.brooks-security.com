# Verified case library: governing AI development and deployment (Domains III and IV)

Worked, citable cases for Chapters 8 to 13 of the reference companion. Every row was checked against the source named in it. Each summary is written in our own words.

Coverage note: 22 cases in the main table. Chapters 8 to 10 span Domain III (governing AI development: design and impact assessment, training and testing data, release and monitoring). Chapters 11 to 13 span Domain IV (governing AI deployment and use: the decision to deploy, assessing what you adopt, and operating, monitoring and retiring).

| SyRI welfare fraud risk scoring (Netherlands) | Rechtbank Den Haag, ECLI:NL:RBDHA:2020:865 (English summary ECLI:NL:RBDHA:2020:1878) | 5 February 2020 | https://www.rechtspraak.nl/organisatie-en-contact/organisatie/rechtbanken/rechtbank-den-haag/nieuws/syri-wetgeving-in-strijd-met-het-europees-verdrag-voor-de-rechten-voor-de-mens | III.A design and impact assessment; III.B data dependency | The court held that the SyRI legislation breached the fair balance required by Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, because the system's use was insufficiently transparent and verifiable. It is the clearest illustration that a group-level harm, scored through data that encodes socioeconomic and migration patterns, can be struck down even when no individual complaint would have surfaced it. |
| Rotterdam welfare fraud algorithm ("Gekleurde technologie") | Rekenkamer Rotterdam, report "Gekleurde technologie" | April 2021 | https://rekenkamer.rotterdam.nl/docs/onderzoeken/R.P.20.06-gekleurde-technologie.pdf | III.A design and impact assessment; IV.C monitoring and oversight | Rotterdam's own Court of Audit found the city had insufficient oversight of how algorithms were used, that caseworkers were unaware of how their work fed the algorithm, and that the data's reliability had not been established. The city paused use of its welfare fraud algorithm in 2021. It shows a design and oversight failure caught by an auditor rather than by the model's own monitoring. |
| Dutch childcare benefits scandal (toeslagenaffaire) | Amnesty International, "Xenophobic Machines" (report); Dutch government resignation | 15 January 2021 (resignation); October 2021 (report) | https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2021/10/xenophobic-machines-dutch-child-benefit-scandal/ | III.A design and impact assessment; III.B data provenance and representativeness | A risk classification system used from 2013 by the Dutch tax authority flagged childcare benefit claims for fraud using nationality and dual citizenship as signals, and thousands of families were wrongly accused. Amnesty documented how the discrimination was built into the design rather than emerging from the model. The scandal forced the resignation of the entire Dutch government in January 2021, showing how a design and data failure in a public welfare system becomes a national political crisis. |
| Drivers v. Uber and Ola (robo-firing) | Gerechtshof Amsterdam (Amsterdam Court of Appeal), three linked judgments | 4 April 2023 | https://fountaincourt.uk/2023/04/amsterdam-court-upholds-appeal-in-algorithmic-decision-making-test-case-drivers-v-uber-and-ola/ | III.A autonomy and human oversight; IV.C governance of automated decisions | The court found that the automated deactivation of drivers' accounts was automated decision-making within the meaning of Article 22 GDPR, and that the companies' claim of meaningful human review did not hold. The cases were brought by driver groups supported by the Worker Info Exchange and the ADCU. It shows that an opaque, largely automated termination pipeline is a governance and data protection failure, not just an operational one. |
| Bauserman v. Michigan Unemployment Insurance Agency (MiDAS) | Class action settlement, Michigan Court of Claims, Bauserman v. State of Michigan Unemployment Insurance Agency | Final approval 29 January 2024 (press release 30 January 2024) | https://www.michigan.gov/ag/news/press-releases/2024/01/30/class-action-settlement-approved-by-court-of-claims | III.A design and impact assessment; III.C incident handling | Michigan's automated fraud-detection system (MiDAS) wrongly accused more than 3,000 people of unemployment insurance fraud, triggering seizures and collections. The state settled the class action for roughly 20 million dollars. It is a design-stage failure, where a model was allowed to raise fraud findings and trigger enforcement without adequate oversight, that ended in mass remediation. |
| Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme (Australia) | Report of the Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme, Commissioner Catherine Holmes AC SC | Presented 7 July 2023 | https://robodebt.royalcommission.gov.au/publications/report | III.A design and impact assessment; IV.C incident handling | The Royal Commission examined an automated income-averaging scheme that raised welfare debts, later found unlawful, and reported on the human harm it caused. It found the scheme prioritised debt recovery over legality and care. It is a worked example of an impact assessment that was never done, and of how an automated decision pipeline removed human judgement from a consequential process. |
| Amazon recruiting tool (historical bias) | Reuters, "Amazon scraps secret AI recruiting tool that showed bias against women", by Jeffrey Dastin | 10 October 2018 | https://www.reuters.com/article/business/amazon-scraps-secret-ai-recruiting-tool-that-showed-bias-against-women-idUSL2N1VB1FQ/ | III.B training data, historical bias, fairness testing | Amazon built a machine-learning tool to screen resumes and found it penalised women's resumes and downgraded graduates of women's colleges, because it had learned from a decade of male-dominated hiring data. The company scrapped the project. It is the canonical example of historical bias, where a model faithfully learns the pattern in its training data and reproduces the discrimination already present in the world. |
| Optum health risk algorithm (Obermeyer et al.) | Science, "Dissecting racial bias in an algorithm used to manage the health of populations", doi 10.1126/science.aax2342 | 25 October 2019 | https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aax2342 | III.B data, labels and representativeness; IV.C fairness monitoring | The study found that a widely used commercial algorithm, later identified in reporting as Optum's, used health costs as a proxy for health needs, so Black patients at the same risk score were sicker than White patients. Correcting the target so that it measured need rather than cost was estimated to more than double the number of Black patients identified for extra care. It is the standard case on measurement bias and the choice of label, showing that the proxy, not the algorithm, carried the harm. |
| Apple Card underwriting review | New York State Department of Financial Services, Report on Apple Card Investigation | 23 March 2021 | https://www.dfs.ny.gov/reports_and_publications/press_releases/pr202103231 | III.B bias testing; IV.B assurance of an adopted model | After 2019 complaints that Goldman Sachs gave women lower credit limits on the Apple Card, DFS reviewed underwriting data for about 400,000 New York applicants and found no fair lending violation. The report concluded more broadly that credit scoring models and the anti-discrimination laws governing credit access need modernising. It shows an assurance examination into a third-party underwriting model, and that a finding of no violation can still carry a regulatory finding about the model's transparency. |
| Clearview AI facial recognition database | Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (Dutch DPA), decision to impose fines and orders on Clearview AI Inc. | 3 September 2024 | https://www.autoriteitpersoonsgegevens.nl/en/documents/decision-fine-clearview-ai | III.B data provenance and lawful basis | The Dutch data protection authority fined Clearview AI 30.5 million euro and imposed orders subject to penalty for unlawful collection of facial images. The decision turns on the absence of a lawful basis for building the database. It is a provenance case, where the governance failure sits in how training data was collected rather than in how the model performs. |
| Microsoft Tay chatbot | BBC News, "Tay: Microsoft issues apology over racist chatbot fiasco" | 25 March 2016 (launched 23 March 2016) | https://www.bbc.com/news/technology-35902104 | III.C pre-deployment testing and red teaming | Microsoft launched a conversational bot on Twitter that learned from interactions with users and within about a day was posting inflammatory and offensive content, forcing the company to shut it down and apologise. The gap was adversarial and misuse testing: nobody had modelled what hostile users would do to a learning system after release. It is the textbook pre-deployment testing failure for a generative system. |
| Tesla Autopilot recall 23V-838 and NHTSA follow-up | NHTSA Part 573 Safety Recall Report 23V-838; NHTSA ODI Resume, Recall Query RQ24009 | Recall filed 12 December 2023; recall query opened 25 April 2024 | https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2023/RCLRPT-23V838-8276.PDF | III.C release and monitoring; IV.C corrective action and retirement | Tesla filed a defect report covering 2,031,220 vehicles equipped with Autopilot, describing the system's controls as possibly insufficient to prevent driver misuse, and remedied it through a software update. NHTSA then opened a recall query in April 2024 to assess whether the remedy was adequate, after 20 crash reports. It shows over-trust in an automated feature, a corrective-action release, and a regulator testing whether the fix actually landed. |
| FDA predetermined change control plans for ML-enabled devices | FDA, "Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Device Software Functions: Lifecycle Management and Marketing Submission Recommendations" and the Predetermined Change Control Plan guiding principles | Guidance; content current 18 August 2025 | https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/software-medical-device-samd/predetermined-change-control-plans-machine-learning-enabled-medical-devices-guiding-principles | III.C release gates and change control; IV.A model change | FDA guidance promotes a total product life cycle approach to AI-enabled medical devices and lets manufacturers pre-authorise model changes in a predetermined change control plan rather than returning for approval on every update. The plan fixes what may change, how it will be verified, and how safety will be monitored. It is the clearest regulatory model for the model-change and re-evaluation clauses a deployer should write into a vendor agreement. |
| Zillow Offers wind-down | Zillow Group, Q3 2021 shareholder letter (SEC Form 8-K exhibit 99.3) | 2 November 2021 | https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1617640/000161764021000085/exhibit993.htm | IV.A deployment decision; IV.C retirement and decommissioning | Zillow announced it would wind down Zillow Offers, its algorithmic home-buying business, cutting roughly 25 percent of its workforce, after the business could not accurately forecast future home prices. The valuation model had been plausible case by case and collectively wrong at scale. It is a worked example of a deployment decision that failed on speed and scale, and of a controlled retirement of an AI-driven operation. |
| DORA and NIS2 (third-party ICT risk) | Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (DORA); Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (NIS2) | DORA applies from 17 January 2025; NIS2 transposition deadline 17 October 2024 | https://www.eiopa.europa.eu/digital-operational-resilience-act-dora_en | IV.B vendor contracts and third-party risk | DORA requires financial entities to manage ICT third-party risk, including concentration risk and a defined set of key contractual provisions for ICT providers, and NIS2 requires supply chain security between an entity and its suppliers. Together they are the EU baseline for the vendor-contract terms a deployer must secure before adopting a third-party system. They show that monitoring, audit rights and exit terms are now a legal expectation, not a negotiating preference. |
| UK Post Office Horizon scandal | Post Office Horizon IT Inquiry, Volume 1 of the Final Report, chaired by Sir Wyn Williams | 8 July 2025 | https://www.postofficehorizoninquiry.org.uk/news/chair-calls-swift-action-deliver-full-and-fair-compensation-urgent-report | IV.C monitoring, automation bias and incident handling | Horizon, an accounting system built by Fujitsu, produced apparent shortfalls that the Post Office treated as reliable, and hundreds of sub-postmasters were wrongly accused of theft and fraud. The inquiry, spanning 225 days of hearings and 298 witnesses, described it as one of the UK's biggest miscarriages of justice. It is the deepest case on automation bias and over-trust: staff and management deferred to the system over the people it accused, and the errors went undetected for years. |
| Change Healthcare ransomware attack | U.S. Congressional Research Service, "The Change Healthcare Cyberattack and Response Considerations for Policymakers", IN12330 | Attack disclosed 21 February 2024; report updated 24 April 2024 | https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/HTML/IN12330.web.html | IV.B third-party dependency; IV.C incident management and breach notification | A ransomware attack on Change Healthcare, a unit of UnitedHealth Group, disrupted healthcare payments nationwide and drew a HIPAA investigation from HHS. The incident concentrated an outage across the sector because so much of the claims pipeline ran through one provider. It shows third-party concentration risk and the mechanics of a major incident response, from federal coordination to the call for clearer reporting duties. |
| Garante v. OpenAI (ChatGPT) | Garante per la protezione dei dati personali, decision closing the OpenAI investigation | 20 December 2024 | https://www.garanteprivacy.it/home/docweb/-/docweb-display/docweb/10085432 | III.B data protection in training; IV.C incident response and disclosure | Italy's data protection authority fined OpenAI 15 million euro and required a six month public information campaign, finding breaches that included a transparency failure toward users. The action followed a 2023 order that had temporarily blocked ChatGPT in Italy. It is a worked example of a regulator treating training data and user disclosure as a governance incident, with both a financial sanction and a mandated communication remedy. |
| Moffatt v. Air Canada | 2024 BCCRT 149, British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal | 14 February 2024 | https://www.canlii.org/en/bc/bccrt/doc/2024/2024bccrt149/2024bccrt149.html | IV.C external communication and accountability | An airline chatbot told a customer about a bereavement fare policy that did not exist, the customer relied on it, and the tribunal held the airline liable for negligent misrepresentation, ordering it to pay about 812 Canadian dollars. The airline had argued that the chatbot was a separate legal entity responsible for its own statements, and the tribunal rejected that. It shows that an organization answers for what its system tells customers, and that a wrong answer is an incident the organization must own. |
| KPMG Australia achieves ISO/IEC 42001 certification | KPMG Australia and BSI Australia media release | 17 October 2024 | https://kpmg.com/au/en/media/media-releases/2024/10/kpmg-first-company-to-achieve-ai-management-system-standard-certification-by-bsi.html | IV.C audit, certification and third-party assurance | KPMG Australia became the first organisation globally certified to ISO/IEC 42001 by the certification body BSI, after a detailed audit of how it governed AI across the business. The record is public evidence that an AI management system can be independently assessed and certified. It is a concrete example of the third-party assurance the BOK asks for, and of what certification to the standard looks like in practice. |
| NYC Local Law 144 enforcement audit | New York State Comptroller, Office of the State Comptroller, audit 2024-N-6, "Enforcement of Local Law 144" | 2 December 2025 | https://www.osc.ny.gov/state-agencies/audits/2025/12/02/enforcement-local-law-144-automated-employment-decision-tools | IV.C audits, assurance and regulatory oversight | The New York State Comptroller audited whether New York City's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection effectively enforced Local Law 144, the city's bias audit law for automated employment decision tools. It shows the gap between a bias-audit duty on paper and an auditor's view of whether it changes behaviour. |
| FTC v. Rite Aid Corporation | Federal Trade Commission, press release and consent order | 19 December 2023 | https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/12/rite-aid-banned-using-ai-facial-recognition-after-ftc-says-retailer-deployed-technology-without | IV.C oversight, automation bias and corrective action | The FTC found that Rite Aid's facial recognition system falsely tagged shoppers as shoplifters, disproportionately affecting women and people of colour, and that the company had not implemented reasonable procedures to prevent harm. ## UNVERIFIED, excluded

The following were investigated and kept out of the main table because a primary or sufficiently reliable source could not be confirmed.

- A named vendor model-deprecation incident. A concrete case of a vendor retiring a model or API and the deployer's contractual notice rights being tested was not found with a primary source, so the model-change and deprecation implication is carried by the FDA predetermined change control plan row and the DORA and NIS2 row instead.
- A standalone model-card failure or enforcement action. No case was found in which a regulator acted specifically on a deficient model card, so the model-card competency is not represented by its own row.
- NIS2 as a distinct case entry. NIS2 is a directive rather than an incident, so it is cited within the DORA row rather than given its own row.

## Coverage gaps

- Monitoring and drift detection. No single primary source was found that documents a named production system in which drift detection triggered a retraining or rollback in a way that can be cited cleanly. The nearest verified material is the Post Office Horizon inquiry (errors undetected over years) and the Tesla recall query (a regulator testing whether a corrective release worked), so degradation and failure to detect are covered by failure cases rather than by a positive drift-detection case.
- External communications and crisis response. Coverage rests on Moffatt v. Air Canada and the Dutch childcare benefits resignation, both of which are about accountability and public reaction rather than a documented, disciplined pre-planned AI disclosure programme. A case with a published post-incident communications plan was not located.
