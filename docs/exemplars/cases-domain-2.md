# Verified case library: privacy law and the law that already reached AI (Domain II)

Worked, citable cases for Chapters 4 and 5 of the reference companion. Every row was checked against the source named in it, and each summary is written in our own words rather than copied from the source.

Coverage note: 18 cases in the main table. Rows 1 to 7 span Domain II.A (how existing data privacy laws apply to AI: transparency, choice, lawful basis, and controller duties including automated decision-making). Rows 8 to 18 span Domain II.B (how other existing laws apply to AI: intellectual property, non-discrimination, consumer protection and product liability).

| SCHUFA scoring judgment | Court of Justice of the European Union (First Chamber), Case C-634/21, OQ v Land Hessen (SCHUFA Holding AG) | 7 December 2023 | https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62021CJ0634 | II.A controller duties, automated decision-making (GDPR Article 22) | The Court held that the automated establishment of a credit probability value by a credit information agency is itself an Article 22(1) decision where the recipient draws strongly on that value to establish, implement or terminate a contractual relationship. Because the score is the decision, the agency has to satisfy one of the Article 22(2) exceptions and provide the Article 22(3) safeguards. It is the authority for the proposition that automated decision duties travel up the vendor chain, a pattern that applies to any upstream model or scoring provider. |
| Dun and Bradstreet Austria | Court of Justice of the European Union (First Chamber), Case C-203/22, CK v Magistrat der Stadt Wien (Dun and Bradstreet Austria GmbH) | 27 February 2025 | https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex:62022CJ0203 | II.A transparency, right of access to the logic of an automated decision (GDPR Article 15(1)(h)) | The Court held that a person subject to automated decision-making is entitled to a concise and intelligible explanation of the procedure and principles actually applied, sufficient to understand and challenge the outcome. It rejected the idea that an algorithm dump or a summary of general categories would do. Where the controller claims trade secrets, it must disclose the reasoning to the supervisory authority or court, which then balances the competing interests. |
| Garante v OpenAI (ChatGPT) | Garante per la protezione dei dati personali, decision No. 755 of 2 November 2024 closing the OpenAI investigation, announced 20 December 2024; annulled by the Court of Rome, judgment No. 4153/2026 | 20 December 2024 (annulled March 2026) | https://www.garanteprivacy.it/home/docweb/-/docweb-display/docweb/10085432 | II.A lawful basis, transparency, supervisory authority enforcement | Italy's data protection authority fined OpenAI 15 million euro and required a six month public information campaign, finding a breach of the transparency principle and the related information duties, plus an absent age verification system. The Garante's own page records that the decision was removed from its website after the Court of Rome annulled the fine in 2026. It is a worked example of both the reach of a national authority over a generative AI service and the volatility of that enforcement on appeal, which is the practical reason to reason from the doctrine rather than the fine. |
| Garante v Luka Inc. (Replika) | Garante per la protezione dei dati personali, order against Luka Inc. of 10 April 2025, announced 19 May 2025 | 10 April 2025 | https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/ai-the-italian-supervisory-authority-fines-company-behind-chatbot-replika_en | II.A lawful basis, data protection by design and by default (GDPR Article 25), transparency, vulnerable users | The Italian authority fined Luka Inc. 5 million euro for breaches of Articles 5(1)(a), 6, 12, 13, 5(1)(c), 24 and 25(1). It found that the company had no identified legal basis for processing through the Replika companion chatbot, an inadequate privacy policy, and no age verification mechanism at registration or during use. The authority opened a separate proceeding on the lawfulness of processing across the whole lifecycle of the generative system. It shows that design and default duties, not only notices, attach to a consumer generative AI product. |
| Clearview AI facial recognition database | Garante per la protezione dei dati personali, decision of 10 February 2022, announced 9 March 2022; Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (Dutch DPA), decision of 16 May 2024, published 3 September 2024 | 9 March 2022 | https://www.garanteprivacy.it/home/docweb/-/docweb-display/docweb/9751323 | II.A special category data (biometrics), lawful basis, multi-authority enforcement | The Italian authority fined Clearview AI 20 million euro, ordered erasure of data on people in Italy and banned further processing, finding that biometric and geolocation data drawn from web scraping was processed without a valid legal basis and that the company's commercial interest did not qualify as legitimate. The Dutch authority later fined the same company 30.5 million euro on comparable findings. It is the clearest illustration that biometric templates scraped at scale trigger the Article 9 prohibition, and that several authorities will pursue the same conduct in parallel. |
| Garante v Foodinho (Glovo) | Garante per la protezione dei dati personali, order against Foodinho S.r.l. of 5 July 2021, with a further order of 22 November 2024 imposing a 5 million euro fine | 5 July 2021 | https://www.garanteprivacy.it/home/docweb/-/docweb-display/docweb/9677377 | II.A data protection impact assessment (Article 35), automated decision safeguards, minimisation, records of processing | The Italian authority fined Foodinho 2.6 million euro over a rider management algorithm covering roughly 19,000 riders in Italy, finding breaches of Articles 22(3), 5(1)(a), 5(1)(c), 5(1)(e), 13, 25, 30, 32, 35 and 37(7) GDPR. The company lacked procedures allowing riders to obtain human intervention, express a view or contest automated decisions that could exclude them from work, and it had not carried out an adequate impact assessment for high risk processing. It is the leading case on impact assessment and contestability in algorithmic management, and the first involving joint cooperation with another supervisory authority under the GDPR. |
| Meta Ireland data transfers | Data Protection Commission (Ireland), decision IN-20-8-1 into Meta Platforms Ireland Limited, announced 22 May 2023, following EDPB Binding Decision 1/2023 | 22 May 2023 | https://www.dataprotection.ie/news-media/press-releases/Data-Protection-Commission-announces-conclusion-of-inquiry-into-Meta-Ireland | II.A international transfers (GDPR Articles 44 to 49), post-Schrems II safeguards | Ireland's supervisory authority fined Meta 1.2 billion euro over the legal basis for transferring EU user data to the United States, and ordered the transfers to be suspended. The European Data Protection Board's binding decision drove the outcome. It is not an AI case, and that is the point: any AI deployment that centralises training or inference in a non-EU cloud runs on this framework, so standard contractual clauses must be paired with a transfer impact assessment of the destination's surveillance law and any supplementary measures. |
| Bartz v. Anthropic PBC | U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (order on fair use, 787 F. Supp. 3d 1007); final approval of class settlement, 20 July 2026 | 23 June 2025 (fair use order); 20 July 2026 (final approval) | https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.434709/gov.uscourts.cand.434709.231.0_5.pdf | II.B intellectual property, training data acquisition and fair use | The court held that training a language model on lawfully acquired books was transformative fair use, but refused to excuse Anthropic's separate downloading and retention of a permanent library of pirated books. The case settled for 1.5 billion US dollars, with final approval granted on 20 July 2026. The split is the teaching point: how the training corpus was acquired can be unlawful even where the training itself is fair use, so provenance is a diligence question a customer must ask. |
| Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence | U.S. District Court for the District of Delaware, No. 1:20-cv-613-SB, opinion of Judge Bibas on fair use | 11 February 2025 | https://www.ded.uscourts.gov/sites/ded/files/opinions/20-613_5.pdf | II.B fair use, training data, market substitution | The court granted summary judgment for Thomson Reuters on direct copyright infringement, rejecting Ross's fair use defence for training a legal research tool on Westlaw headnotes. The use was not transformative because Ross built a product that substituted for the original, and the effect on the market weighed against the defence. It is the counterpoint to the fair use wins and shows that the fourth factor, market harm from competing output, is where a commercial training use most often fails. |
| Getty Images v. Stability AI | High Court of Justice of England and Wales, Chancery Division, [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch), Mrs Justice Joanna Smith | 4 November 2025 | https://www.judiciary.uk/judgments/getty-images-v-stability-ai/ | II.B model does not store copies, territoriality of training, text and data mining questions | The court held that a diffusion model's trained parameters are not an "infringing copy" of the training images, because the weights do not store or reproduce a recognisable copy of any work. Getty had accepted that training and development took place outside the United Kingdom, so the court did not decide a UK training infringement claim at all, and the only findings against Stability were limited historic trade mark breaches over watermarks in outputs. It shows that copyright is territorial, that where training happened decides which law applies, and that output watermarking is a separate trade mark exposure that survives an IP claim. |
| Thaler v. Perlmutter | U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, No. 23-5233, 130 F.4th 1039; certiorari denied, No. 25-449 | 18 March 2025 (certiorari denied 2 March 2026) | https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2025/03/23-5233.pdf | II.B output ownership and human authorship | The D.C. Circuit held that the Copyright Act requires a work authored in the first instance by a human being, so an AI system cannot itself be the author of a copyrightable work. The Supreme Court declined to review the decision. It is the authority on output ownership: a prompt alone is not authorship, though human selection, arrangement and modification of AI output can be protected, which is why applicants must disclose and exclude AI generated material. |
| GEMA v. OpenAI | Regional Court of Munich I, Case No. 42 O 14139/24 | 11 November 2025 | https://cms.law/en/deu/legal-updates/gema-vs.-openai-munich-regional-court-i-issues-landmark-copyright-decision | II.B memorisation, reproduction in model and output, operator responsibility | The court essentially upheld the German collecting society's claims for injunctive relief, information and damages, holding that song lyrics memorised in the model parameters, and reproduced in outputs and stored in chat histories, were reproductions of the protected works. It also placed responsibility for outputs generated from simple prompts on the operator rather than the end user. Read with Getty, it shows the live divergence on whether a model contains copies, and it is the clearest European decision that the text and data mining exception does not extend to memorisation. |
| Mobley v. Workday, Inc. | U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, No. 3:23-cv-00770-RFL, order granting preliminary collective certification | 16 May 2025 | https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.408645/gov.uscourts.cand.408645.128.0.pdf | II.B non-discrimination, Title VII, the ADA, vendor-supplied screening tools | The court conditionally certified a nationwide collective in a suit alleging that Workday's AI based applicant screening system discriminated against job applicants on the basis of race, age and disability. The certified collective covers applicants aged 40 and over, with the race and disability claims continuing. It shows that an employer is exposed for the screening tool it adopts even when a vendor built it, and that a vendor's platform can itself be the target of a discrimination claim. |
| Obermeyer et al., racial bias in a health algorithm | Science, "Dissecting racial bias in an algorithm used to manage the health of populations", 366(6464), doi 10.1126/science.aax2342 | 25 October 2019 | https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aax2342 | II.B algorithmic discrimination findings, proxy and label bias | The study found that a widely used commercial risk scoring algorithm predicted health care costs rather than illness, so at any given risk score Black patients were measurably sicker than White patients. Correcting the target to measure need rather than cost was estimated to raise the share of Black patients identified for extra care from 17.7 percent to 46.5 percent. It is the standard demonstration that discrimination can enter through the choice of label or proxy rather than through a protected attribute, which is the mechanism behind the facially neutral features examined in fair lending and employment. |
| Apple Card underwriting review | New York State Department of Financial Services, report on the Apple Card investigation and its underwriter, Goldman Sachs Bank USA | 23 March 2021 | https://www.dfs.ny.gov/reports_and_publications/press_releases/pr202103231 | II.B fair lending, ECOA, assurance of a third-party underwriting model | After complaints that Goldman Sachs gave women lower credit limits on the Apple Card, the department reviewed underwriting data for about 400,000 New York applicants and found no fair lending violation. The report nonetheless concluded that credit scoring in its current form, and the anti-discrimination laws governing credit access, need strengthening and modernisation, and it noted that the model's complexity made the allegation hard to test. It shows an assurance examination of a third-party underwriting model, and that a no violation finding can still carry a regulatory finding about transparency and explainability. |
| FTC Operation AI Comply | Federal Trade Commission, press release announcing five law enforcement actions | 25 September 2024 | https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes | II.B consumer protection, FTC Act section 5 deception and deceptive AI claims | The Commission brought five actions against operators that used AI hype or sold AI tools capable of deceptive use, including DoNotPay's claim to offer "the world's first robot lawyer". The stated position is that there is no AI exemption from the laws already on the books, so an overstated capability claim is a deceptive act whether or not the product is AI. It is the anchor case for substantiating every performance claim before launch, with section 5 unfairness and state UDAP analogues sitting beside it. |
| Product Liability Directive | Directive (EU) 2024/2853 of the European Parliament and of the Council on liability for defective products, repealing Directive 85/374/EEC | Adopted 23 October 2024, published 18 November 2024, transposition deadline 9 December 2026 | https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/2853/oj/eng | II.B product liability, software and AI as a product, defect in an adaptive system | The Directive brings software, including AI systems, within the definition of a product irrespective of the mode of its supply or usage, so software stored on a device, delivered over a network or offered as a service is covered. Liability is no fault, evidence disclosure can be ordered once a claim is plausible, and defect or causation can be presumed where technical complexity makes proof excessively difficult, with machine learning named as an example. Its defect test takes account of a product's ability to continue learning after it is placed on the market, and liability to an injured person cannot be contracted out. |
| Benavides v. Tesla, Inc. | U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Florida, No. 1:21-cv-21940-BLOOM/Torres, jury verdict | 1 August 2025 | https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/59932667/benavides-v-tesla-inc/ | II.B product liability, defective driver assistance software | A Florida jury found Tesla partly at fault in a fatal crash involving its Autopilot driver assistance feature, with reporting placing Tesla's share at about 243 million US dollars of a total award of roughly 329 million dollars. Tesla has appealed. It is the worked example of a defective software theory against an automated feature: the claimant did not have to prove negligence, only defect, damage and causation, and the allocation of fault between the system and the human driver became the contested question. |

## UNVERIFIED, excluded

The following were investigated and kept out of the main table because no primary or sufficiently reliable source could be confirmed, or because the claim could not be matched to a checkable record.

- A Latvian data protection authority fine concerning ethnicity inferred from a surname. Searches did not return a primary decision, a case reference or a reliable report naming the controller, the date and the amount together, so the inferred special category point is carried by the Clearview row and by the Obermeyer row instead. The underlying doctrine, that an AI can reconstruct a special category attribute that was never collected, is stated in Chapter 4 from the GDPR text and EDPB Opinion 28/2024 rather than from this case.
- Any figure for the OpenAI fine that assumes it stands. The 15 million euro fine was annulled by the Court of Rome in 2026, so the amount is recorded in the table only with the annulment beside it.
- Any case resting on a fault based AI liability presumption. No injury case decided under such a presumption exists to cite, because the proposed AI Liability Directive was never adopted, so Chapter 5 rests that point on the revised Product Liability Directive and national tort law.

## Coverage gaps

- Consumer protection stands on a single row. Section 5 deception about AI capability is well evidenced, but no verified CFPB action applying UDAAP specifically to an AI product was found, so the unfairness and abusiveness limb is covered by the statutory text in Chapter 5 rather than by a case.
- International transfers rests on a case that is not itself an AI matter, the Meta Ireland decision. The doctrine applies directly to AI deployments that centralise processing outside the EEA, but no AI specific transfer enforcement with a citable decision was located.
- Special category and inferred data has no European decision squarely on inference of a protected trait by a model. Clearview establishes the prohibition for scraped biometrics, and the Obermeyer study establishes proxy encoding of race, but the middle case, a regulator finding that a model inferred a special category attribute from ordinary inputs, was not found in verifiable form.
