# OECD AI Principles — Structured Reference

> **Full Title:** Recommendation of the Council on Artificial Intelligence
> **Legal Instrument:** OECD/LEGAL/0449
> **Status:** Non-binding recommendation (political commitment)
> **Originally Adopted:** 22 May 2019
> **Revised:** 8 November 2023 (AI system definition); 3 May 2024 (substantive updates)

---

## Overview

The **OECD Recommendation on Artificial Intelligence** is the **first intergovernmental standard on AI**. Adopted by the OECD Council at Ministerial level in May 2019, it was revised in 2023 and 2024 to reflect technological developments — including generative AI — and to strengthen implementation guidance.

The Recommendation has **two complementary sections**:
1. **Five value-based principles** for responsible stewardship of trustworthy AI (applicable to all stakeholders)
2. **Five national policy recommendations** for governments and international co-operation

> The G20 Leaders welcomed the **G20 AI Principles** at the June 2019 Osaka Summit — these were drawn directly from the OECD Recommendation, giving the principles global reach beyond OECD membership.

## Key Definitions

### AI System
> *"An AI system is a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments. Different AI systems vary in their levels of autonomy and adaptiveness after deployment."*

**2023 update clarified:** objectives may be explicit or implicit; inputs may come from humans or machines; outputs include "content" (capturing generative AI); "physical" replaced "real" for clarity; systems can continue to evolve after deployment.

### AI System Lifecycle
Phases — often iterative, not necessarily sequential:
1. Plan and design
2. Collect and process data
3. Build model(s) and/or adapt existing model(s) to specific tasks
4. Test, evaluate, verify and validate
5. Make available for use / deploy
6. Operate and monitor
7. Retire / decommission

### AI Actors
Organisations and individuals who play an active role in the AI system lifecycle, including those who **deploy or operate** AI.

### AI Knowledge
Skills and resources — data, code, algorithms, models, research, know-how, training programmes, governance, processes, best practices — required to understand and participate in the AI system lifecycle.

### Stakeholders
All organisations and individuals involved in, or affected by, AI systems, directly or indirectly. AI actors are a subset of stakeholders.

## Section 1: The Five Principles for Trustworthy AI

> The principles are **complementary** and should be considered as a **whole**.

### 1.1 — Inclusive Growth, Sustainable Development and Well-Being

> *"Stakeholders should proactively engage in responsible stewardship of trustworthy AI in pursuit of beneficial outcomes for people and the planet."*

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Human augmentation** | Augmenting human capabilities and enhancing creativity |
| **Inclusion** | Advancing inclusion of underrepresented populations |
| **Inequality reduction** | Reducing economic, social, gender, and other inequalities |
| **Environmental sustainability** | Protecting natural environments *(explicit reference added in 2024 revision)* |
| **Overall aim** | Invigorating inclusive growth, well-being, and sustainable development |

### 1.2 — Respect for the Rule of Law, Human Rights and Democratic Values, Including Fairness and Privacy

> *"AI actors should respect the rule of law, human rights, democratic and human-centred values throughout the AI system lifecycle."*

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Foundational values** | Non-discrimination and equality, freedom, dignity, autonomy of individuals, privacy and data protection, diversity, fairness, social justice, internationally recognised labour rights |
| **Information integrity** | Addressing misinformation and disinformation amplified by AI, while respecting freedom of expression and other protected rights *(2024 addition)* |
| **Safeguards and mechanisms** | Capacity for human agency and oversight; addressing risks from uses outside of intended purpose, intentional misuse, or unintentional misuse *(2024 addition)* |

### 1.3 — Transparency and Explainability

> *"AI Actors should commit to transparency and responsible disclosure regarding AI systems."*

**Four disclosure obligations** (provide meaningful information, appropriate to context, consistent with state of the art):

| # | Obligation |
|---|-----------|
| **i** | Foster a **general understanding** of AI systems, including their capabilities and limitations |
| **ii** | Make stakeholders **aware of their interactions** with AI systems, including in the workplace |
| **iii** | Where feasible and useful, provide **plain and easy-to-understand information** on data sources/input, factors, processes and/or logic that led to the prediction, content, recommendation or decision — enabling those affected to understand the output |
| **iv** | Provide information enabling those **adversely affected** by an AI system to **challenge its output** |

### 1.4 — Robustness, Security and Safety

> *"AI systems should be robust, secure and safe throughout their entire lifecycle."*

| Sub-principle | Description |
|---------------|-------------|
| **a) Normal, foreseeable, and adverse conditions** | AI systems should function appropriately and not pose unreasonable safety and/or security risks under normal use, foreseeable use or misuse, or other adverse conditions |
| **b) Override, repair, decommission** | Mechanisms should be in place to ensure that if AI systems risk causing undue harm or exhibit undesired behaviour, they can be overridden, repaired, and/or decommissioned safely as needed *(2024 strengthening)* |
| **c) Information integrity** | Where technically feasible, mechanisms should bolster information integrity while ensuring respect for freedom of expression *(2024 addition)* |

### 1.5 — Accountability

> *"AI actors should be accountable for the proper functioning of AI systems and for the respect of the above principles."*

| Sub-principle | Description |
|---------------|-------------|
| **a) Role-based accountability** | AI actors should be accountable based on their roles, the context, and consistent with the state of the art |
| **b) Traceability** | Ensure traceability of datasets, processes, and decisions made during the AI system lifecycle — to enable analysis of outputs and responses to inquiry *(moved from Transparency in 2024 revision)* |
| **c) Systematic risk management** | Apply a systematic risk management approach to each phase of the AI system lifecycle on an **ongoing basis**; adopt responsible business conduct via co-operation between AI actors, suppliers of AI knowledge/resources, AI system users, and other stakeholders *(2024 elaboration)* |
| **Risk scope** | Risks include harmful bias, human rights (safety, security, privacy), labour rights, and intellectual property rights |

## Section 2: National Policies and International Co-operation

> Consistent with the five principles. Special attention to **small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs)**.

### 2.1 — Investing in AI Research and Development

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Public & private investment** | Long-term public investment and encouragement of private investment in R&D and open science |
| **Interdisciplinary focus** | Spur innovation in trustworthy AI focused on challenging technical issues AND AI-related social, legal, ethical implications and policy issues |
| **Open-source tools & datasets** | Public and private investment in open-source tools and open datasets that are representative and respect privacy and data protection — to support R&D free of harmful bias and improve interoperability |

### 2.2 — Fostering an Inclusive AI-Enabling Ecosystem

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Digital ecosystem** | Foster development of, and access to, an inclusive, dynamic, sustainable, and interoperable digital ecosystem for trustworthy AI |
| **Infrastructure components** | Data, AI technologies, computational and connectivity infrastructure, mechanisms for sharing AI knowledge |
| **Data trusts** | Promote mechanisms such as data trusts to support safe, fair, legal, and ethical sharing of data |

### 2.3 — Shaping an Enabling Interoperable Governance and Policy Environment for AI

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Agile policy** | Promote an agile policy environment supporting the transition from R&D to deployment and operation |
| **Experimentation** | Use experimentation (e.g., regulatory sandboxes) to provide controlled environments for testing and scaling AI systems |
| **Outcome-based approaches** | Adopt approaches providing flexibility in achieving governance objectives |
| **Interoperability** | Co-operate within and across jurisdictions to promote interoperable governance and policy environments *(2024 emphasis)* |
| **Regulatory review** | Review and adapt policy and regulatory frameworks and assessment mechanisms to encourage innovation and competition for trustworthy AI |

### 2.4 — Building Human Capacity and Preparing for Labour Market Transformation

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Workforce preparation** | Work with stakeholders to prepare for transformation of the world of work and society; empower people to effectively use and interact with AI systems |
| **Skills** | Equip people with necessary skills across the breadth of AI applications |
| **Fair transition** | Ensure a fair transition for workers — training programmes along working life, support for those displaced (including social protection), access to new labour market opportunities |
| **Responsible use at work** | Promote responsible use of AI at work — enhance worker safety, quality of jobs and public services, foster entrepreneurship and productivity, ensure benefits are broadly and fairly shared |

### 2.5 — International Co-operation for Trustworthy AI

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Active co-operation** | Governments (including developing countries) and stakeholders should actively co-operate to advance these principles and progress responsible stewardship |
| **Knowledge sharing** | Work together in the OECD and other global/regional fora to foster sharing of AI knowledge |
| **Multi-stakeholder initiatives** | Encourage international, cross-sectoral, and open multi-stakeholder initiatives to garner long-term expertise on AI |
| **Global technical standards** | Promote development of multi-stakeholder, consensus-driven global technical standards for interoperable and trustworthy AI |
| **Measurement** | Encourage development and government use of internationally comparable indicators to measure AI R&D and deployment, and to assess progress in implementation |

## 2024 Revision: Key Updates

| Area | What Changed |
|------|-------------|
| **Information integrity** | New emphasis on addressing misinformation and disinformation, safeguarding information integrity in the context of generative AI |
| **Misuse** | Explicit coverage of uses outside intended purpose, intentional misuse, and unintentional misuse |
| **Transparency disclosure** | Clarified the specific information AI actors should provide about AI systems |
| **Safety mechanisms** | Strengthened language: if AI systems risk undue harm or exhibit undesired behaviour, they must be overridable, repairable, and/or decommissionable safely |
| **Responsible business conduct** | Emphasised throughout the AI system lifecycle, with co-operation among all AI actors, suppliers, users, and stakeholders |
| **Interoperable governance** | Underscored need for jurisdictions to work together on interoperable governance, given the increase in AI policy initiatives worldwide |
| **Environmental sustainability** | Explicit reference added — reflecting growing importance since 2019 |
| **Traceability → Accountability** | Traceability and risk management text moved from elsewhere to the Accountability principle as the most appropriate home |

## Implementation Mechanisms

### OECD.AI Policy Observatory
Launched February 2020. An inclusive hub for public policy on AI that aims to help countries encourage, nurture, and monitor responsible development of trustworthy AI. Features:
- **Live database** of AI strategies, policies, and initiatives (countries and stakeholders can share and update)
- **Interactive comparison** of key elements across jurisdictions
- **AI metrics, measurements, policies, and good practices** — continuously updated
- **Practical guidance for implementation** — iterated based on Observatory findings

### ONE AI (OECD Network of Experts on AI)
- Informal group of AI experts from government, business, academia, and civil society
- Provides AI-specific policy expertise and advice to the OECD
- Space for in-depth discussions about shared AI policy opportunities and challenges

### Working Party on AI Governance (AIGO)
- Created 2022 under the Digital Policy Committee (DPC)
- Supports implementation of the Recommendation
- Develops practical guidance for implementation
- Provides a forum and online hub for exchanging information on AI policy
- Fosters multi-stakeholder and interdisciplinary dialogue through ONE AI Expert Groups

### Reporting Cycle
- DPC instructed to report to Council on implementation, dissemination, and continued relevance
- **Next report:** No later than five years following the 2024 revision, and at least every ten years thereafter

## Comparison with Other Major Frameworks

### OECD AI Principles vs. NIST AI RMF

| Dimension | OECD AI Principles | NIST AI RMF |
|-----------|--------------------|-------------|
| **Nature** | Intergovernmental policy recommendation (non-binding) | Voluntary technical framework |
| **Scope** | 40+ OECD member and adhering countries | Primarily U.S. but adopted globally |
| **Audience** | Governments + all AI actors (developers, deployers, operators) | AI developers, deployers, evaluators, users |
| **Structure** | 5 principles + 5 policy recommendations | 4 core functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage |
| **Approach** | Values-based, high-level principles | Process-based, operational risk management |
| **Accountability** | Role-based; traceability + systematic risk management | Govern function establishes accountability structures; RACI model |
| **Transparency** | Four disclosure obligations (general understanding → challenge outputs) | Map function documents context; Measure evaluates transparency |
| **Robustness/Safety** | Override/repair/decommission mechanisms | Manage function: risk treatment, incident response, system retirement |
| **Update cycle** | Revised 2023, 2024 (political process) | Continuously updated (Playbook, profiles, ARIA) |
| **Implementation tools** | OECD.AI Observatory, practical guidance | AI RMF Playbook, ARIA testing, model cards |

> **Complementary relationship:** The OECD Principles provide the **normative foundation** (the "why"), while the NIST AI RMF provides the **operational methodology** (the "how"). Organisations often map OECD principles to NIST AI RMF subcategories for comprehensive governance.

### OECD AI Principles vs. ISO/IEC 42001

| Dimension | OECD AI Principles | ISO/IEC 42001 |
|-----------|--------------------|---------------|
| **Nature** | Intergovernmental recommendation | International certifiable standard |
| **Binding?** | No (political commitment) | Yes, if organisation seeks certification |
| **Structure** | 5 principles + 5 policy recommendations | Annex SL management system (Context → Leadership → Planning → Support → Operation → Performance → Improvement) |
| **Focus** | What governments and AI actors *should do* | How organisations *should manage* AI systems |
| **Trustworthiness** | Five principles define trustworthiness dimensions | Trustworthiness dimensions required as considerations: transparency, fairness, robustness, accountability, privacy |
| **Alignment** | Foundational values | Operational governance system |
| **Certification** | N/A | Certifiable by accredited bodies |
| **Implementation** | Via national policy, OECD.AI guidance | Via documented AI management system, internal audit, management review |

> **Layered approach:** The OECD Principles set the **policy direction** → ISO 42001 provides the **management system** to operationalise those principles → NIST AI RMF provides the **risk management processes** to identify, assess, and treat AI risks on an ongoing basis.

### OECD Influence on Other Frameworks

| Framework | OECD Influence |
|-----------|---------------|
| **G20 AI Principles** (2019) | **Directly drawn** from the OECD Recommendation |
| **EU AI Act** | OECD principles influenced the Act's risk-based approach; transparency and accountability requirements reflect OECD language |
| **G7 Hiroshima Process** (2023) | Code of Conduct for advanced AI builds on OECD principles — risk lifecycle, transparency, content provenance |
| **UNESCO AI Ethics Recommendation** (2021) | Overlapping human rights and human-centred values, though UNESCO goes further on cultural diversity |
| **Council of Europe AI Treaty** | References OECD as foundational intergovernmental standard |

## Key Principles at a Glance

| # | Principle | One-Line Summary |
|---|-----------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Inclusive Growth, Sustainable Development, Well-Being** | AI should benefit people and the planet |
| 2 | **Human Rights, Democratic Values, Fairness, Privacy** | AI must respect the rule of law, human dignity, and fundamental rights |

## Exam Relevance (AIGP)

- **Domain II, Subdomain II.D** — "Understand the main industry standards and tools that apply to AI"
- The OECD Principles are **not legally binding** but are the **most influential** international AI governance framework — they shaped the EU AI Act, G7/G20 AI policies, and national AI strategies worldwide
- Know the **five principles** by name and their core thrust
- Understand that the 2024 revision added: **information integrity** (misinformation/disinformation), **misuse** (intentional/unintentional), **environmental sustainability**, and strengthened **safety override mechanisms**
- Expect questions on how OECD compares to NIST AI RMF and ISO 42001 — OECD = foundational policy direction; NIST = operational risk management; ISO 42001 = certifiable management system
